## Just Diagnosed, and Not Sure What Comes Next

The appointment wraps up, sleep apnea is now in your chart, and you walk out to the parking lot holding a printout and a dozen half-formed thoughts. By the time you get home, the questions finally arrive: what does this mean for my nights, what are my options, and where do I even start?

Knowing the right questions to ask after a sleep apnea diagnosis can turn a vague, overwhelming moment into a clear plan. Fall is a natural time to settle that plan, too, while there is still room on the calendar before the year wraps up.

At Austin Sleep & Airway Health, we work alongside sleep physicians to help patients understand their options, especially oral appliance therapy. This guide gives you a calm, stage-by-stage list of questions to bring to your physician, your dentist, and your insurance plan.

## Questions to Ask After a Sleep Apnea Diagnosis About Your Results

Start with the diagnosis itself. Your physician diagnosed you through a sleep study, and that report holds more than a single label. If the numbers on it feel like a foreign language, our guide to [reading your sleep study results](/sleep-study-results-explained/) walks through AHI, oxygen, and the severity ranges in plain terms, so we will not repeat them here.

Instead, focus on what the numbers mean for you. Patient advocacy groups such as Apnea Partners suggest asking questions like these:

- What type of sleep apnea do I have: obstructive, central, or a mix?
- How would you describe my severity, and what in my report drove that?
- Was my breathing worse on my back or during a particular stage of sleep?
- Was this from a home sleep test or an in-lab study, and do I need another?
- Is anything else, such as nasal blockage or another health condition, likely playing a role?

## Which Sleep Apnea Treatment Fits Me?

This is the question most people really want answered. Ask your physician to walk you through the reasonable options for your type of sleep apnea and why one fits you best, then ask what happens if the first option does not work out.

### CPAP

The National Heart, Lung, and Blood Institute describes positive airway pressure as the most common treatment, and MedlinePlus calls CPAP the most effective approach for many people. It is a well-established, first-line option. Ask which mask styles you can try and who to call if the fit feels off. Adjusting takes time for many people, and if that is you, our guide for people [struggling with CPAP](/struggling-with-cpap/) covers practical next steps.

### Oral Appliance Therapy

A custom oral appliance holds the lower jaw slightly forward during sleep to help keep the airway open. The joint clinical practice guideline from the American Academy of Sleep Medicine and the American Academy of Dental Sleep Medicine recommends that sleep physicians consider an oral appliance for adults with obstructive sleep apnea who are intolerant of CPAP or prefer an alternative, and it suggests a custom, adjustable device fitted by a qualified dentist. Your physician decides whether it fits your situation.

### Using Both

Some people do not have to choose. Ask whether a combination makes sense, such as CPAP at home and an appliance for travel or camping. We cover that approach in more detail in our post on [using CPAP and an oral appliance together](/cpap-and-oral-appliance-together/).

## How Will We Know the Treatment Is Working?

How you feel matters, and so does objective confirmation. Ask your physician how success will be measured, and when.

For CPAP, that often includes reviewing usage and data from the machine at follow-up visits. For oral appliance therapy, the AASM and AADSM guideline suggests follow-up sleep testing once the appliance has been adjusted, to confirm how well it is working, along with periodic visits to both the dentist and the sleep physician. It is also fair to ask which side effects to watch for and what your dentist will monitor over time.

Also ask how often you will follow up, and whether your sleep physician or primary care doctor will manage this long term.

## Questions to Ask Your Insurance Plan

Coverage varies widely, so the most reliable answers come from your plan itself. Call the number on your card and ask: Does my plan cover CPAP, an oral appliance, or both for obstructive sleep apnea? Is prior authorization required? Are there usage requirements to meet? Is the supplier or dentist in network, and what out-of-pocket costs should I expect?

Fall is a good moment for this call, since many people want to understand their plan year before it resets. Note the date and a reference number. Our post on [insurance and oral appliance therapy](/insurance-oral-appliance-sleep-apnea/) explains common terms so the call feels less confusing.

## What You Can Do Now: Daily Habits and What to Track

While you and your physician settle on a plan, a few steady habits are worth a look. NHLBI lists regular physical activity, a consistent sleep schedule, a healthy weight, limiting alcohol and caffeine, and not smoking among lifestyle changes that can help. MedlinePlus also notes that side sleeping can help some people with milder obstructive sleep apnea.

It also helps to keep a simple two-week log: bedtime, wake time, how rested you feel each morning, any morning headaches or dry mouth, and anything a bed partner notices. Bring that log, your sleep study report, and your list of questions to every visit. Patterns are easier to talk through on paper than from memory.

## When It's Worth an Evaluation for Oral Appliance Therapy

An evaluation is worth considering if your physician has mentioned an oral appliance as an option, if you are finding CPAP hard to use most nights, or if you travel often and want a backup plan. It is also worth a look if you want to understand all of your choices first.

An evaluation does not lock you into anything. It helps you learn whether your teeth, jaw, and airway are a good match for an appliance, so you and your physician can decide together.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### What should I ask my doctor after being diagnosed with sleep apnea?

Ask what type and severity of sleep apnea you have, which treatments fit your results, how success will be measured, and when follow-up testing will happen. Writing these down before your visit helps you leave with a clear plan rather than more questions.

### Is CPAP my only option for sleep apnea?

No, CPAP is an effective first-line option, but physicians may also discuss oral appliance therapy, positional strategies, lifestyle changes, or surgery depending on your situation. Your physician weighs your study results, anatomy, and preferences when recommending a path.

### Can a dentist treat sleep apnea?

A trained dentist can fit and adjust a custom oral appliance once a physician has diagnosed sleep apnea and prescribed the device. The dentist and physician then work together, with follow-up testing to confirm how well the appliance is working.

### How do I know if my sleep apnea treatment is working?

Your physician confirms it with objective data, such as CPAP usage reports or a follow-up sleep study with your oral appliance in place. How rested you feel, along with notes from a bed partner, adds helpful context at each follow-up.

### Do I need another sleep study after getting an oral appliance?

Yes, AASM and AADSM guidance suggests a follow-up sleep test after the appliance has been adjusted, to confirm how well it is working. Your physician decides whether a [home sleep test or in-lab study](/home-sleep-test-vs-lab-study/) is the better fit for that check.
