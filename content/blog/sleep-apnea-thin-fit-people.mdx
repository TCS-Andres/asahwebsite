## The Fittest Snorer in Your Running Group

It's 5:45 on a Saturday, and your group is stretching for the long run around Lady Bird Lake. Your fall training block is on track. Yet your partner mentioned, again, that you snored last night, and you woke up feeling like you had already run the miles.

If that sounds familiar, you may be wondering about sleep apnea in thin people, and whether it could apply to you. The usual picture of sleep apnea doesn't look like a lean runner, and that's why it can be missed.

At Austin Sleep & Airway Health, we look at the airway itself. This post explains why a fit body can still have a snug airway, where weight fits in, what to track this training season, and when an evaluation is worth it.

## Why Sleep Apnea in Thin People Often Gets Missed

Yes, you can have sleep apnea if you're thin and fit. Excess weight is one of the best-known risk factors, but not the only one. The National Heart, Lung, and Blood Institute (NHLBI) notes that genes help determine the size and shape of your skull, face, and upper airway, and that sleep apnea can run in families.

The stereotype is the problem. Lean adults often assume snoring can't mean anything, and the tiredness gets filed under training fatigue. Yet one study in the Journal of Clinical Sleep Medicine found that about one in four people diagnosed with obstructive sleep apnea at a hospital sleep clinic had a body mass index in the normal range.

Our guide to [how snoring differs from sleep apnea](/snoring-vs-sleep-apnea/) covers that distinction. Here, the focus is anatomy.

## What Causes Sleep Apnea If You're Not Overweight?

During sleep, the muscles that hold your airway open relax, and whether it stays open depends on how much room there was to begin with. Training changes your heart and legs, not the bones and soft tissue that frame your airway.

A review of X-ray studies in Sleep Medicine Reviews found that, on average, adults with obstructive sleep apnea showed differences in lower jaw length and position and a narrower airway behind the tongue and palate.

### Jaw Size and Position

A smaller or set-back lower jaw leaves less room for the tongue, so it sits closer to the throat. MedlinePlus lists a lower jaw that is short compared with the upper jaw as one contributing feature. The upper jaw forms both the roof of the mouth and the floor of the nose, so a narrow palate can mean less room in both places.

### Tongue Size and Resting Posture

A tongue that is large for its space, or one that rests low instead of up against the palate, can fall back toward the throat during sleep. A 2020 study of people with obstructive sleep apnea and a lower BMI found the base of the tongue was the most common site of narrowing on their backs, and it often eased on their side.

### Soft Palate, Tonsils, and Adenoids

The American Academy of Otolaryngology describes a long soft palate acting like a flutter valve during relaxed breathing, which is the sound we call snoring. Large tonsils and adenoids can crowd the same space in adults, not only in children.

### Nasal Passages

A deviated septum, swollen nasal tissue, or allergy congestion makes the nose harder to breathe through, and ENT specialists note it takes extra effort to pull air past. Many people shift to mouth breathing at night, which can let the jaw drop and the tongue slide back. Fall allergy season can make this more noticeable.

Researchers also describe traits unrelated to size, like how strongly throat muscles respond in sleep and how easily you wake.

## Does Weight Still Matter If You're Already Lean?

Yes. Weight remains one of the most consistent risk factors for obstructive sleep apnea, and NHLBI notes that maintaining a healthy weight can help prevent or treat sleep apnea linked to excess weight. A long-running Wisconsin study published in JAMA found that moderate weight gain over four years was linked with more breathing events during sleep, and moderate loss with fewer.

Still, weight doesn't explain everyone. A lean person with a set-back jaw and a blocked nose may have less airway room than a heavier person with a wide palate. So the useful question isn't whether you're thin enough to rule this out. It's how much room your airway has once you're asleep.

## What to Track During Your Fall Training Block

You already log miles and splits. A few weeks of night notes can be just as useful at a visit:

* Snoring: ask a partner or record a few nights with your phone, and note whether it's worse on your back.
* Pauses, gasps, or choking sounds someone else hears.
* How you wake: dry mouth, morning headache, or a tight, tired jaw.
* Your nose: stuffy at night, worse with allergies, or mouth breathing even on easy runs.
* Patterns after a post-race drink, hard training days, or travel.
* Family history of snoring or sleep apnea, plus your history with braces, tonsils, or nasal injuries.

## When Snoring in a Fit Body Is Worth an Evaluation

It's worth a look if you snore most nights, especially if someone notices pauses or gasping, or if you wake unrefreshed despite consistent sleep and sensible training. Morning headaches, recurring dry mouth, and recovery that lags behind your training are worth mentioning to your doctor too. So is a family history of sleep apnea paired with a set-back jaw, a narrow palate, or a nose that rarely feels clear.

Only a physician can diagnose sleep apnea, through a home sleep test or an in-lab sleep study, and our comparison of [home sleep tests and in-lab studies](/home-sleep-test-vs-lab-study/) explains both. None of these signs is a verdict on your fitness. They're simply information.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Can you have sleep apnea if you're not overweight?

Yes, people at a normal weight can have obstructive sleep apnea, because jaw size, tongue position, tonsils, nasal blockage, family history, and age all shape the airway. MedlinePlus lists a large tongue and large tonsils among the features that can narrow it.

### Is sleep apnea in thin people usually the central type?

Not usually, because obstructive sleep apnea, where the upper airway narrows or closes during sleep, is the most common type overall, according to NHLBI. Central sleep apnea means the brain does not send the signals needed to breathe, and a sleep study tells the two apart.

### Why do I snore if I'm not overweight?

Snoring happens when relaxed tissue vibrates as air squeezes through a narrowed space, and a long soft palate, large tonsils, or a stuffy nose can narrow it at any weight. Back sleeping and evening alcohol can make it more noticeable.

### Can running or regular exercise make sleep apnea go away?

Exercise can help support better sleep, but it cannot reshape your jaw, palate, or nasal passages, so it isn't a reason to skip an evaluation if symptoms continue. A 2014 meta-analysis in the journal Lung found supervised exercise modestly lowered sleep apnea severity in small groups of adults, with little weight change.

### Can a young, healthy adult have sleep apnea?

Yes, sleep apnea can show up in young, healthy adults, although NHLBI notes that risk tends to rise with age and that it is more common in men. In younger, fit adults, enlarged tonsils, a narrow palate, or a set-back jaw can be part of the picture.
