## Can a Tongue Tie Cause Neck Pain? Where the Idea Comes From

A tongue tie, or ankyloglossia, happens when the lingual frenulum, the band of tissue under the tongue, is short or tight enough to limit how far the tongue lifts or moves. Mainstream descriptions of tongue tie, including MedlinePlus, focus on feeding, speech, and swallowing. Neck or shoulder tension is not part of that traditional picture.

The idea that a tied tongue could contribute to neck tension comes mostly from clinicians who work with posture, breathing, and orofacial function: physical therapists, myofunctional therapists, and some dentists. The reasoning is anatomical. The tongue attaches to the hyoid bone, which connects through muscles to the jaw and the neck, so a tongue that can't rest or move normally may ask nearby muscles to compensate, including muscles tied to head position and swallowing.

This is a hypothesis some clinicians and patients describe from what they notice in practice, not an established medical finding. We think it's worth explaining honestly, including what current research does and doesn't show.

## How a Tied Tongue Might Affect Posture and Muscle Tension

### Forward Head Posture

One idea clinicians describe is that a tongue resting low in the mouth, rather than against the roof of the mouth, may go along with the head sitting slightly forward of the shoulders. Forward head posture on its own is well studied: a systematic review found a meaningful association with chronic neck pain, describing tighter upper trapezius and neck muscles alongside weaker deep neck muscles. That review did not study tongue tie specifically, and the authors note the underlying studies are still limited in size and length.

### Jaw and Swallowing Patterns

A restricted tongue can also change how someone swallows, sometimes recruiting the jaw, lips, or neck muscles instead of the tongue doing that work alone. Some clinicians describe this repeated compensation, happening with every swallow, as a plausible way tension could build in the jaw and upper neck over time, a pattern related to what we cover in [jaw clicking and popping](/jaw-clicking-and-popping/). This is a broader pattern, not a confirmed cause and effect.

## What the Research Actually Shows

Direct research on tongue tie and neck pain specifically is limited. One study that measured head forward posture in children being evaluated for sleep disordered breathing risk found that head forward posture was more common among children flagged for higher risk. When researchers in that same study looked specifically at frenulum length, though, they did not find a clear relationship between a short lingual frenulum and head forward posture.

That doesn't rule out a connection, but it does mean the broader claim that tongue ties cause neck and shoulder pain is running ahead of the evidence. The resting tongue posture ideas in our piece on [mewing versus myofunctional therapy](/mewing-vs-myofunctional-therapy/) come from a similar place: a plausible mechanism that research hasn't caught up to yet. As of this writing, we haven't found a published position from a major sleep or ENT authority connecting tongue tie to neck or shoulder pain.

## What You Can Track Now

If you're curious whether your own tongue plays a role in your neck tension, you don't need special equipment, just a couple of weeks of noticing. Track when your neck and shoulder tension tends to flare, whether that's mornings, after long calls, or after meals. Notice whether your tongue rests against the roof of your mouth or sits low during the day, whether you catch yourself clenching or grinding at night, and whether you breathe mostly through your nose or your mouth.

Bring these notes to whoever you see next, whether that's a physical therapist, your dentist, or an airway evaluation, so they have real patterns to work from instead of a single visit's snapshot.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Can a tongue tie actually cause neck or shoulder pain?

There's no strong research confirming that a tongue tie directly causes neck or shoulder pain, though some clinicians describe a possible connection through posture and muscle compensation. The idea is more common in physical therapy and myofunctional circles than in mainstream medical literature. If you notice both a tight tongue and ongoing neck tension, it's worth mentioning to whoever you see for either concern.

### What is the theory behind tongue tie and neck tension?

The theory is that a tongue tied to the floor of the mouth changes resting posture and swallowing, which may ask nearby jaw and neck muscles to work harder. The tongue attaches to the hyoid bone, and hyoid muscles connect to both the jaw and the neck, which is the anatomical basis clinicians point to. It's a plausible mechanism, not a proven one.

### Is there research connecting tongue tie to posture or neck pain?

Very little research looks at tongue tie and neck pain specifically, and the one study designed to test that link in children did not find a clear connection. Broader research on forward head posture and neck pain shows a real association, but that research isn't specific to tongue tie. We rely on what's published rather than filling a gap with a guess.

### Can releasing a tongue tie relieve my neck pain?

There's no research showing that releasing a tongue tie reliably relieves neck or shoulder pain, so we don't present a release as a treatment for it. Some people do notice looser jaw and neck muscles once the tongue moves more freely and myofunctional therapy retrains its resting posture, but that's an individual report, not something we promise. If neck pain is your main concern, a physical therapist is often the more direct place to start.

### What else besides a tongue tie can cause neck and shoulder tension?

Neck and shoulder tension usually has more common causes than a tongue tie, including desk posture, stress, old injuries, and sleep position. A tongue tie, if present, is more likely to be one contributing factor among several rather than the whole explanation. That's part of why we look at the tongue alongside the rest of the airway and jaw exam, not on its own.
