## The Little Box on the Nightstand

Ragweed is back, your nose feels tight by bedtime, and your partner has started nudging you around 2 AM. So you grab a box of nasal strips for snoring on the way home, press one on before bed, and hope for a quieter night.

Sometimes it helps. Other times nothing changes, or the snoring fades while you still wake up tired. Both make sense once you know what these products actually do.

At Austin Sleep & Airway Health, we hear questions about strips, internal dilators, and saline every fall. This guide explains what each product category can do for nasal airflow and snoring, where its limits are, and when persistent snoring is worth a closer look.

## Why Fall in Central Texas Makes Your Nose Feel Smaller

Cedar gets the headlines in winter, but ragweed pollen in Central Texas typically runs from late summer into fall, often peaking around late September and October.

Lying down adds to the problem. Cleveland Clinic explains that when you are upright, gravity helps mucus drain, but once you lie flat it tends to pool, and indoor allergens such as dust and pet dander can add to nighttime stuffiness.

When the nose narrows, many people shift to mouth breathing during sleep, often with louder snoring and a dry mouth by morning. Our posts on [mouth taping for sleep](/mouth-taping-for-sleep/) and [cedar season congestion and snoring](/cedar-fever-and-snoring/) cover related ground.

## Do Nasal Strips for Snoring Actually Help?

For some people, yes. External nasal strips are adhesive bands with a springy layer that gently lifts the sides of the nose, widening the nasal opening from the outside.

A small study published in the journal Rhinology in 1997 followed 35 habitual snorers who had already been checked with an overnight sleep study and did not have sleep apnea. After two weeks of nightly strip use, bed partners rated snoring as significantly lower, and participants reported less mouth dryness and less daytime sleepiness. It had no comparison group, so it points in a helpful direction rather than proving a result.

Notice who was studied: snorers without sleep apnea. Strips tend to help most when the front of the nose is the bottleneck. They cannot change what happens further back, at the soft palate, tongue, or jaw.

## Nasal Strips vs. Internal Nasal Dilators: Which Works Better?

Internal dilators, sometimes called nasal vents or cones, are small soft devices that sit just inside the nostrils and hold the opening wider from within. Strips pull from the outside; dilators push from the inside.

Direct comparisons are limited. A 2019 open study in Acta Biomedica compared an internal dilator with an external strip in 41 people who snore and found that both reduced snoring time and improved perceived sleep quality, with the internal device helping somewhat more people in that group.

In practice, the choice often comes down to comfort: some people find adhesive irritates their skin, while others dislike something inside the nostril. If neither changes much, that suggests the front of the nose may not be the main source of the noise.

## Can Nasal Strips or Dilators Treat Sleep Apnea?

No, they are not a treatment for obstructive sleep apnea. A 2016 systematic review and meta-analysis in Pulmonary Medicine pooled studies of both external strips and internal dilators. It found that while these products improved nasal breathing, they did not meaningfully change the apnea hypopnea index (AHI), lowest oxygen levels, or snoring measures in people with sleep apnea.

That is why a quieter night can be misleading. A strip can make snoring quieter while the pauses in breathing that define sleep apnea continue underneath. Quieter snoring does not rule sleep apnea out. Our guide to [snoring versus sleep apnea](/snoring-vs-sleep-apnea/) walks through that difference in more detail.

Sleep apnea is diagnosed only by a physician, through a home sleep test or an in-lab study, as the National Heart, Lung, and Blood Institute explains. If you already use CPAP or another therapy, keep following your care plan.

## Is a Saline Spray or Rinse OK to Use Every Night?

Saline sprays and rinses are salt water with no medication in them. Cleveland Clinic lists saline among the comfort measures for nighttime stuffiness.

If you use a rinse bottle or pot, the FDA advises using only distilled, sterile, or previously boiled and cooled water, never plain tap water, and cleaning and drying the device after each use. How often saline makes sense for you, and whether anything beyond saline is appropriate, is a good question for your physician or allergist. Saline can make a stuffy nose more comfortable, but it does not change your airway's shape or treat sleep apnea.

## What You Can Track This Fall

If you are experimenting with strips, dilators, or saline, a short log turns guesses into something useful. For two weeks, note which product you used, how your nose felt at bedtime, and whether you woke with a dry mouth. Ask a bed partner about snoring and any pauses or gasping, or use an overnight phone recording.

Note daytime energy and morning headaches, high pollen days, and whether one nostril always feels more blocked. A consistently one-sided blockage is covered in our post on [deviated septum and sleep](/deviated-septum-and-sleep/).

Bring the log, and any past sleep study results, to your next visit.

## When Snoring Is Worth an Evaluation

Snoring that comes and goes with a cold or a high pollen week is common. It is worth a look if snoring stays loud most nights even with a strip or dilator, if a partner notices pauses or gasping, or if you feel unrefreshed after a full night in bed.

It is also worth mentioning to your doctor if snoring continues after allergy season or daytime fatigue affects work or driving. These are not a diagnosis, just signals that a closer look could help.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Do nasal strips actually help snoring?

Nasal strips can reduce snoring for some people, especially when a narrow front of the nose is part of the problem. A small 1997 study of snorers without sleep apnea found partners rated snoring lower after two weeks, though results vary widely from person to person.

### Nasal strips vs. internal nasal dilators, which works better?

Neither is clearly better for everyone, and research directly comparing them is small and limited. One 2019 study found both reduced snoring time, so comfort, skin sensitivity, and fit often decide which one people prefer.

### Can nasal strips treat sleep apnea?

No, nasal strips and internal dilators are not a treatment for sleep apnea, even when they make snoring quieter. A 2016 review found they improved nasal breathing but did not meaningfully change sleep apnea measures such as the AHI.

### Is saline rinse or spray OK to use every night?

Many people use saline, which contains no medication, as part of a nightly routine, but how often is right for you is a question for your physician or allergist. If you use a rinse device, the FDA advises distilled, sterile, or previously boiled water.

### Why does my nose feel blocked only at night?

Lying flat makes it harder for mucus to drain, and bedroom allergens like dust and pet dander can add to nighttime stuffiness. A nose that stays blocked on one side in every season may point to a structural issue worth mentioning to your doctor.

### What else can I try if strips and saline do not change my snoring?

If nasal aids do not change your snoring, the source is often further back in the airway, such as the soft palate, tongue, or jaw position. Our guide on [how to stop snoring](/how-to-stop-snoring/) covers position, alcohol timing, and professional options worth discussing.
