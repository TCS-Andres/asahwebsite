## When Stress Follows You Into Bed

You wake up with your jaw already tight, a dull ache settling into your temples before your feet even hit the floor. Your partner mentions again that they heard grinding sounds coming from your side of the bed overnight. It has been a stretch of long days, and you are starting to wonder whether your stress is following you straight into sleep.

April is Stress Awareness Month, observed every year since 1992 to highlight how stress shows up in the body. Grinding teeth at night is one of the more common ways stress surfaces while you sleep, though stress rarely tells the whole story on its own.

## Why Do You Grind Your Teeth at Night?

Bruxism is the clinical term for grinding or clenching your teeth, and it can happen while you are awake or asleep. MedlinePlus notes that the exact cause is not fully understood, though daily stress is one of the most commonly cited triggers.

When you are under pressure, your jaw muscles can hold tension the same way your shoulders or neck might. That tension does not always ease once you fall asleep. Grinding tends to occur during lighter stages of sleep, alongside brief, mostly unnoticed awakenings that happen many times a night.

Stress is not the only contributor. The American Dental Association points to other factors, including a bite that does not line up evenly, and notes that people who smoke, drink alcohol, or drink a lot of coffee are more likely to grind their teeth. Genetics and other sleep disorders can play a role too.

## Is Teeth Grinding Linked to Sleep Apnea and Your Airway?

Researchers have long noticed that grinding and sleep disordered breathing often show up in the same people, though the relationship is still being studied. A 2025 narrative review in the Journal of Prosthodontics describes sleep bruxism and obstructive sleep apnea as commonly co-occurring conditions, without settling exactly why.

One proposed explanation involves the brief awakenings, called micro-arousals, that happen when breathing is disrupted during sleep. As the body works to reopen a narrowed airway, jaw muscles can activate along with everything else, and a grinding episode can follow. Under this theory, the grinding is a byproduct of the airway working harder, not the original cause.

This does not mean everyone who grinds their teeth has a breathing problem, and it does not mean airway issues always cause grinding. It does mean that when grinding shows up alongside snoring, gasping, or mouth breathing, the airway is worth mentioning at your next visit. Only a physician can diagnose sleep apnea, through a sleep study.

## Signs You Might Be Grinding Your Teeth at Night

### What You Might Notice in the Morning

A sore or tight jaw on waking, tenderness along your temples, or a dull headache are common. Some people notice an ache that sits closer to the ear, since the jaw joint is right in front of it.

### What a Dentist Might Notice

Flattened, chipped, or worn edges on your teeth are frequent clues, along with a scalloped edge along the tongue or ridges on the inside of your cheeks. These changes build slowly, so you might not spot them yourself.

### What a Partner Might Notice

Grinding often makes an audible sound, and a partner may hear it before you are ever aware of it. Snoring, pauses in breathing, or restless tossing alongside the grinding are worth mentioning together, not separately.

## What You Can Track Now

You do not need special equipment to start paying attention. A simple log for a week or two gives any evaluation more to work with:

- When your jaw feels sorest: on waking, after meals, or late in the day
- Headaches, especially at the temples, and when they start
- Sounds a partner notices, including grinding, snoring, or gasping
- Stress patterns: work deadlines, poor sleep, or a stretch of hard weeks
- Whether you already use a night guard, and how it has been working

Stress management on its own can help ease some of the pressure. Regular sleep, movement, and simple relaxation habits, like slow breathing before bed, may help support a calmer jaw, even though they will not resolve every case.

## When It's Worth an Evaluation

Occasional grinding during a stressful week is common and often settles on its own. It is worth a look if grinding happens most nights, if your teeth are visibly wearing down, or if jaw pain is starting to get in the way of eating or talking comfortably.

It is also worth mentioning to your doctor if grinding shows up alongside snoring, morning fatigue, or witnessed pauses in breathing. None of these signs confirm anything on their own, but together they give a physician or dentist useful information to consider.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Why do I grind my teeth at night when I'm stressed?

Stress is one of the most common triggers for nighttime grinding, since jaw muscles can hold tension the same way your shoulders or neck do. That tension often carries into sleep, especially during lighter sleep stages, and shows up as clenching or grinding you are not aware of.

### Is grinding my teeth a sign of sleep apnea?

Grinding does not confirm sleep apnea on its own, but the two are frequently studied together and often occur in the same people. If grinding shows up alongside snoring, gasping, or daytime tiredness, it is worth mentioning to your doctor, who can determine whether a sleep study makes sense.

### Will a night guard stop sleep apnea related grinding?

A standard night guard protects your teeth from wear, but it does not hold your airway open the way a mandibular device or CPAP can. If sleep apnea is contributing to your grinding, treating the airway is usually a separate conversation from protecting your teeth.

### Can I wear a night guard along with CPAP?

Many people use a night guard and CPAP together, though the fit and materials matter, especially around a mask seal. Talk with your sleep physician and dentist so the two devices work well side by side.

### How can I tell if I am grinding my teeth at night?

Morning jaw soreness, temple headaches, and flattened or chipped teeth are common signs, along with grinding sounds a partner might notice. Many people only find out after a dentist spots the wear during a routine visit.

### Does stress alone explain teeth grinding, or could something else be going on?

Stress is a common trigger, but it is rarely the only factor, since bite alignment, sleep patterns, and airway health can all play a part. A thorough evaluation looks at the whole picture rather than assuming stress explains everything.
