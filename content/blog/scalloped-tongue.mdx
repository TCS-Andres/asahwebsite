## Noticing Ridges Along the Edge of Your Tongue

You are brushing your teeth, or checking a sore spot with the bathroom mirror, when you notice it: the edges of your tongue have a wavy, ridged pattern, almost like the imprint of a rope along its sides. Nothing hurts. It just looks different than you remember, and once you notice it, it is hard to stop checking. This pattern has a name, a scalloped tongue, sometimes called a crenated tongue, and it shows up because the tongue has been pressing against the teeth long enough to leave a mark.

A scalloped tongue on its own does not diagnose anything, but it is a physical clue that can point toward how you breathe, sleep, and hold your jaw overnight, when none of it is something you are consciously managing.

At Austin Sleep & Airway Health, we notice scalloped tongues often during airway focused exams, and patients usually ask the same two questions: what caused it, and does it mean anything serious. This guide covers the common causes, what research says about the sleep apnea connection, and what is worth tracking before your next appointment.

## What Causes a Scalloped Tongue?

Most of the time, a scalloped tongue develops because the tongue is resting somewhere other than up against the roof of the mouth, or because it does not have quite enough room for where it wants to sit. When the tongue sits low and forward instead of tucked behind the top teeth, its edges settle against the lower teeth night after night and slowly take on their shape.

A few habits and patterns show up again and again alongside a scalloped tongue. Mouth breathing instead of nasal breathing changes where the tongue naturally rests. Restricted tongue mobility from an oral tie can keep the tongue from lifting fully to the palate. Clenching or grinding the jaw, often during sleep, adds steady pressure against the tongue's edges. A narrow palate or a crowded bite leaves less room overall, so the tongue settles against the teeth instead of the roof of the mouth.

A scalloped tongue can also point to something that has nothing to do with the airway. Dehydration, allergies, thyroid changes, and low vitamin levels can all cause mild tongue swelling that presses the edges into the teeth, according to the Cleveland Clinic. If your ridges appeared quickly or came with symptoms like fatigue, mention the combination to your physician rather than assuming it is airway related.

## Is a Scalloped Tongue a Sign of Sleep Apnea?

This is the question most people are really asking, and the honest answer is that a scalloped tongue is a clue, not a diagnosis. Only a physician can diagnose obstructive sleep apnea, and only through a sleep study.

Researchers have looked at this exact pattern for about two decades. A 2005 study in Otolaryngology, Head and Neck Surgery examined patients already being evaluated for snoring and found that a scalloped tongue showed up more often among people whose sleep testing later turned up abnormal breathing, though it was not present in everyone with sleep apnea and did appear in some people without it. A larger community study of more than a thousand adults in Japan, part of the Toon Health Study published in the Journal of Oral Rehabilitation, linked scalloped tongue with nighttime dips in blood oxygen even after accounting for body weight. A more recent study in the journal Frontiers in Sleep found that among patients who had already completed sleep studies, a scalloped tongue was associated with more severe obstructive sleep apnea, though it did not predict who had sleep apnea in the first place.

Put together, the research suggests a scalloped tongue is worth paying attention to, especially alongside snoring, daytime tiredness, or a partner who mentions gasping or pauses in breathing. ## What You Can Track If You Notice a Scalloped Tongue

A little tracking goes a long way before an appointment, and none of it requires special equipment. For a week or two, notice whether your mouth is open or closed as you fall asleep and when you wake up, and whether your tongue rests against the roof of your mouth or falls low and forward during the day.

Write down whether you wake with a dry mouth or sore jaw, whether you catch yourself clenching during the day, and whether a partner mentions snoring, pauses in breathing, or restlessness. Note how your nose feels at bedtime, since chronic congestion often pushes people toward mouth breathing without them noticing. ## When a Scalloped Tongue Is Worth an Airway Evaluation

A scalloped tongue that shows up alongside snoring, morning headaches, jaw soreness, or daytime grogginess is worth a closer look, even before any formal diagnosis. The same is true if it appeared around the same time as new mouth breathing, a partner noticing gasping, or teeth grinding that seems to be getting worse.

An evaluation also makes sense if the ridges have been there for months without other symptoms and you would simply like to understand why. When muscle habits play a role, retraining the tongue and airway muscles through [myofunctional therapy](/myofunctional-therapy-sleep-apnea/) is sometimes part of a plan alongside CPAP or an oral appliance. As Dr. Culotta puts it, "Positive change is possible at any age. Awareness is the first step."

## Frequently Asked Questions

### What does it mean if the sides of your tongue are scalloped?

Scalloped edges usually mean the tongue has been pressing against the teeth long enough to leave a lasting ridge pattern. This can happen because of tongue size, resting posture, jaw clenching, or swelling from causes like allergies or dehydration. It is common and rarely urgent, but worth mentioning at your next dental visit.

### Is a scalloped tongue a sign of sleep apnea?

A scalloped tongue can be one clue among several, but it does not diagnose sleep apnea on its own. Research has linked the pattern to a higher likelihood of abnormal breathing during sleep, especially when it shows up with snoring or daytime tiredness. Only a physician can diagnose sleep apnea, and only through a sleep study.

### Can dehydration cause a scalloped tongue?

Yes, mild dehydration can cause tongue swelling that presses the edges into the teeth and creates a scalloped look. So can allergies, thyroid changes, and certain vitamin levels, according to the Cleveland Clinic. If the ridges appeared quickly or came with other symptoms, it is worth mentioning to your doctor.

### Does a scalloped tongue always mean tongue tie?

No, a scalloped tongue does not always mean an oral tie, though restricted tongue mobility is one common cause. When a tie limits how high the tongue can lift, it tends to rest lower and press against the teeth instead. An airway focused exam checks tongue mobility directly rather than guessing from the ridges alone.

### How do you get rid of a scalloped tongue?

Scalloped tongue ridges often ease once the underlying cause is addressed, whether that is hydration, jaw clenching, or tongue posture. There is no single fix, since the right step depends on what is causing the pressure. A dentist or physician can help sort out which cause applies to you.

### Is a scalloped tongue serious?

A scalloped tongue is rarely serious on its own and does not usually cause pain or other problems by itself. It is still worth mentioning to a dentist or doctor, since it can point toward habits or health changes you would otherwise miss.
