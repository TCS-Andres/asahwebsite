## The School Photo That Made You Look Twice

The fall school picture proofs come home, and there they are again: soft purple-gray shadows under your child's eyes. Your child went to bed on time and slept a full night, so why do they look like they stayed up until midnight?

If you've been wondering about the dark circles under your child's eyes, the calendar may be a clue. Late September sits in the middle of ragweed season in Central Texas, and local allergists say counts usually peak in September and linger until the first frost, the season a stuffy nose shows up on your child's face.

At Austin Sleep & Airway Health, we see dark circles as one piece of a bigger picture: how your child breathes, day and night. Below, you'll learn what usually causes those shadows, how to spot an allergy pattern versus a sleep pattern, and when an airway evaluation is worth your time.

## What Causes Dark Circles Under a Child's Eyes?

In children, dark circles most often come from nasal congestion or family traits, not from staying up too late. SickKids' AboutKidsHealth lists colds, allergies, and enlarged adenoids among the common causes, along with short sleep, dehydration, and inherited pigmentation.

### Congestion and allergic shiners

The skin under the eyes is thin, with small veins just beneath it. A swollen nasal lining slows blood flow through those veins, giving the skin a bluish, bruised look, something allergists call allergic shiners. Cleveland Clinic notes that children are more prone to them than adults.

### Family traits and thin skin

Some children simply have darker under-eye skin because that's how their family is built. If a parent or grandparent has had the same shadows and your child has no stuffy nose or sleep complaints, genetics may explain most of it.

### Short or broken sleep

Too little sleep can make under-eye skin look paler, so the vessels beneath it show through more. A child can still sleep poorly, though, if breathing through the nose is a struggle.

## Are Dark Circles From Allergies or From Being Tired?

Often it's a bit of both, and the two can feed each other. Still, a few patterns help you sort it out.

Allergy clues tend to cluster together. A review of allergic rhinitis in Allergy, Asthma & Clinical Immunology lists persistent mouth breathing, nose rubbing, and dark circles from congestion as outward signs. Sneezing, itchy eyes, and symptoms that flare after time outdoors are common too.

Tiredness clues start with simple math. The American Academy of Sleep Medicine recommends 9 to 12 hours of sleep per 24 hours for children ages 6 to 12. If your child is getting less than that, more time in bed is the easiest first step. If they're already getting enough sleep and still wake up groggy, the question shifts from how long they sleep to how well they breathe while sleeping.

## Can Allergies Affect How Your Child Sleeps and Breathes?

Yes, and this is where dark circles become more than a cosmetic question. When the nose is blocked at night, many children switch to mouth breathing, which can come with snoring, restless tossing, and mornings that start with a short fuse.

A 2013 systematic review found that most of the studies it examined linked allergic rhinitis to sleep-disordered breathing in children, with habitual snoring the most studied sign. The authors rated the evidence as moderate, so it's worth noticing rather than a rule.

Allergies aren't the only source of nighttime congestion. Enlarged adenoids can block airflow too, and the National Heart, Lung, and Blood Institute lists large tonsils or adenoids as a frequent cause of sleep apnea in children. A child whose dark circles never fade, even between pollen seasons, may deserve a look beyond allergies alone.

To be clear, dark circles on their own are not a sign of sleep apnea. They're a hint that the nose may be working harder than it should, and the nose matters a great deal for sleep.

## What You Can Do This Ragweed Season

You can't control the pollen count, but you can reduce how much comes home and gather useful information at the same time. The ACAAI suggests closing windows on high-pollen days and having kids shower after long stretches outdoors. For anything beyond that, ask your physician or allergist about options.

Then, for two to three weeks, keep a simple log:

* A photo of your child's face in the same spot and light each morning, so you can see whether the circles change with the pollen
* Days with sneezing, itchy eyes, nose rubbing, or a stuffy nose
* Whether you see parted lips or hear snoring when you check on them at night
* How hard mornings are, and any teacher comments about energy or focus

If snoring makes your list, our guide to [what child snoring can mean](/child-snoring-what-it-means/) explains which sounds are worth a closer look. Bring the log to your next pediatric visit, since a few weeks of notes beat one tired-looking morning.

## When Are Dark Circles Worth an Evaluation?

Dark circles that come and go with the ragweed and fade after the first frost are often just part of the season. It's worth a closer look if they last for weeks, return every season, or show up alongside snoring, mouth breathing, or a child who wakes tired despite enough hours in bed.

Some situations call for a prompt call to your pediatrician instead: dark circles under only one eye, swelling around the eyes, pale skin with unusual tiredness, or pauses in breathing during sleep.

The American Academy of Pediatrics recommends children be screened for snoring at routine checkups, so your child's next visit is a natural place to bring it up. Only a physician can diagnose sleep apnea, through a sleep study, not a photo or a checklist.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Are dark circles under a child's eyes a sign of something serious?

Usually not, since in most children dark circles come from nasal congestion, family traits, or short sleep rather than a serious health problem. Circles under only one eye, swelling, or paired with pale skin and unusual tiredness are worth a prompt call to your pediatrician.

### Can enlarged adenoids cause dark circles under the eyes?

They can, because enlarged adenoids may block the back of the nose and cause the same congestion that darkens the skin below the eyes. Snoring and mouth breathing are the clues worth writing down if sleep seems disrupted.

### How do you get rid of allergic shiners?

Allergic shiners usually fade as the nasal congestion behind them eases, so the focus is on the congestion rather than the skin itself. Ask your physician or allergist about options, and cut down on indoor pollen by closing windows on high-count days.

### Are dark circles a sign of sleep apnea in kids?

Not on their own, since dark circles are common and have many causes, and only a physician can diagnose sleep apnea through a sleep study. If the circles come with snoring, pauses in breathing, or restless sleep, share that whole pattern with your pediatrician.

### Can dark circles under the eyes be genetic?

Yes, dark circles can run in families, since some children simply have thinner under-eye skin that makes the blood vessels beneath it easier to see. If your child has no stuffy nose, snoring, or daytime tiredness, family traits may explain most of it.
