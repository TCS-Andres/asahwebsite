## The Alarm That Loses Every Morning

Three snoozes, a knock on the door, one more knock, and by the time your teenager is finally upright, you are both running late again. If you are Googling why your teenager is always tired, you are not the only parent doing it this week. Back to school always brings the question back around: is this just what teenagers are like, or is something else going on.

That question deserves a real answer, not a shrug. Some tiredness is expected and biological. Tiredness that does not let up even on a full night's sleep, alongside snoring, mouth breathing, or grinding, is worth a closer look.

This piece from Austin Sleep & Airway Health walks through what typically drives teen tiredness, what is simply part of adolescence, what points toward the airway, what to track before your next appointment, and when it is worth an evaluation.

## Why Is My Teenager Always Tired? Normal Biology or Something More

Most of it starts with biology, not bad habits. Around puberty, the body's internal clock shifts later, delaying when the brain signals it is time to wind down for the night. Sleep researchers describe this as a phase delay, sometimes as much as two hours compared to the middle school years, so a teenager who is wide awake at 10 p.m. is not necessarily choosing to stay up.

Layer an early school start time on top of that biology and the math gets hard. The American Academy of Sleep Medicine recommends that middle and high schools start at 8:30 a.m. or later, in part because most teenagers cannot fall asleep early enough to make up the difference on their own. Screens, sports, homework, and part time jobs push bedtime later still.

None of this means every tired teenager has a medical issue. It means most tiredness has an explanation rooted in a shifting body clock and a schedule not built around it. The pattern is worth noting more when it does not improve even with a chance to sleep in.

## How Much Sleep Does a Teenager Actually Need?

The American Academy of Sleep Medicine recommends 8 to 10 hours of sleep a night for teens ages 13 to 18. Most teenagers fall short of this during the school week and try to make it up later, which does not fully reset a shifted schedule.

Catching up partly works. A teen who sleeps in on Saturday will likely feel sharper by Sunday afternoon. What it does not fully undo is the shifting schedule itself: late nights all week and sleeping until noon on weekends nudge the body clock later again, so Monday feels like starting over.

If your teen is close to 8 to 10 hours most nights and still drags through the day, or falls asleep in the car, in class, or over homework, it is worth looking past the clock for an explanation.

## When Teen Tiredness Points to the Airway, Not Just the Clock

### Snoring, mouth breathing, or grinding at night

Loud or frequent snoring, breathing mostly through an open mouth, or grinding are worth noticing in a teenager the same way they are in a younger child. The American Academy of Pediatrics lists frequent snoring and nighttime breathing problems among the signs that can accompany sleep apnea in kids and teens, alongside daytime sleepiness. If grinding is part of the picture, our guide to [why kids grind their teeth at night](/child-teeth-grinding-at-night/) covers what tends to drive it.

### Morning headaches or a dry, sore throat

Waking up with a headache, a dry mouth, or a sore throat can be tied to breathing through the mouth overnight rather than the nose. We cover this pattern in more detail in [why you wake up with a headache](/waking-up-with-headaches/), and it is worth mentioning at your teen's next dental or medical visit.

### Enlarged tonsils, nasal congestion, or allergies

Allergy season, along with enlarged tonsils or adenoids, can narrow the airway enough to disrupt sleep without anyone realizing why. This does not mean every congested teenager has sleep apnea. It means nasal breathing and tonsil size are worth mentioning if fatigue is persistent.

## What to Track Before Your Teen's Next Checkup

A two-week log turns a vague sense of always tired into specifics a doctor or Dr. Culotta can use. Ask your teen to help, since older kids often notice patterns adults miss.

- **Bedtime and wake time**, on school days and weekends, including how long it actually takes to fall asleep.
- **Nighttime signs**, snoring, mouth breathing, grinding, or gasping, if you or a sibling notice them.
- **Mornings**, whether your teen wakes easily or needs several attempts, and how alert they seem an hour later.
- **Daytime patterns**, falling asleep in class, during car rides, or right after school, plus any headaches or jaw soreness.
- **Screens and caffeine**, when devices go off at night and how much caffeine shows up during the day.

Bring the log to your teen's next pediatrician or dental visit. A consistent bedtime, a cooler dark room, and screens off before lights out are reasonable first steps while you watch for a pattern.

## When a Tired Teenager Is Worth an Evaluation

Tiredness that does not improve with a consistent schedule and enough hours in bed, especially alongside snoring, mouth breathing, morning headaches, or grinding, is worth a conversation with your teen's pediatrician. Only a physician can diagnose sleep apnea, and only through a sleep study.

An airway focused dental evaluation is a useful complement to that conversation, especially when nighttime breathing signs are part of the picture. ## Frequently Asked Questions

### Is it normal for teenagers to be tired all the time?

Some tiredness is normal for teenagers because of a biological shift in their internal clock that delays sleepiness until later at night. It becomes worth a closer look when a teen still seems exhausted despite getting close to 8 to 10 hours of sleep on a regular basis.

### How many hours of sleep does a teenager actually need?

The American Academy of Sleep Medicine recommends 8 to 10 hours of sleep a night for teens ages 13 to 18. Most teenagers fall short of this during the school week and try to make it up later, which does not fully reset a shifted schedule.

### Why does my teen fall asleep in class even after going to bed on time?

A teen can fall asleep in class even after a reasonable bedtime if their sleep is being interrupted overnight without anyone noticing. Snoring, mouth breathing, restless sleep, or an inconsistent schedule can all quietly cut into how restorative that time in bed actually is.

### Is sleeping in all weekend a sign my teen is not getting enough sleep?

Sleeping late on weekends is common when a teen is behind on sleep during the school week and is trying to catch up. If the pattern repeats every week and your teen still seems tired on school days, it is worth tracking bedtime and wake time together.

### What are signs of sleep apnea in a teenager?

Frequent snoring, pauses or gasping during sleep, and problems breathing at night are signs the American Academy of Pediatrics lists alongside daytime sleepiness and trouble concentrating. None of these signs confirm sleep apnea on their own, and only a physician can diagnose it through a sleep study.

### Could my teen's tiredness be something other than a sleep problem?

Persistent fatigue can sometimes come from causes unrelated to sleep, such as anemia, thyroid changes, or mood related concerns your pediatrician can screen for. If sleep and airway signs have already been considered and fatigue continues, that conversation is worth having with your teen's doctor.
