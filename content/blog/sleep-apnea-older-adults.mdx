## The Nap You Didn't Plan to Take

You sat down after lunch to read or catch the news, and forty minutes disappeared before you noticed. It happens more than it used to. Your spouse mentions you dozed off again during the movie last night, and you brush it off. You're retired now, right? Isn't this just what happens?

Sleep apnea in older adults is more common than most people realize, and it often looks nothing like the loud, gasping snoring you might expect. It can show up quietly, as fatigue, foggy thinking, or sleep that never quite feels like enough, and it can be easy to write off as ordinary aging.

September is Healthy Aging Month, a good prompt to look past the assumption that it's just getting older and ask what is actually going on with your sleep. At Austin Sleep & Airway Health, we work with adults of every age to find out. This post covers how sleep apnea can look different later in life, what is worth tracking, and why it is never too late to feel better rested.

## Why Sleep Apnea in Older Adults Often Looks Different

The muscle tone that keeps an airway open changes with age, and research on obstructive sleep apnea in elderly patients has found that loud snoring often becomes quieter over time, even as breathing pauses continue or worsen. If the snoring you or your partner used to joke about has faded, that isn't necessarily good news. The symptom may have simply moved somewhere less obvious.

### Tired in a Different Way

Younger adults with sleep apnea often describe falling asleep at their desk or in traffic. Older adults are more likely to describe trouble falling or staying asleep, morning headaches, low energy, and a foggy, unfocused kind of tired that doesn't match how much time was spent in bed. Women in particular may notice nighttime choking sensations or waking up gasping more than men typically report.

### More Trips to the Bathroom at Night

Waking up two or three times a night to use the bathroom often gets blamed on bladder or prostate changes. Fragmented sleep from a blocked airway can produce that same pattern, especially alongside daytime fatigue or trouble concentrating.

## Could It Be Sleep Apnea, or Just Getting Older?

Occasional forgetfulness is common with age, but ongoing brain fog, trouble concentrating, or new irritability are worth a second look rather than a shrug. Research following adults over 65 has linked untreated sleep apnea and nighttime drops in oxygen to a measurable decline in thinking and memory over time. That doesn't mean every memory lapse points to sleep apnea, only that airway health is worth mentioning alongside other possible causes.

Balance can be part of the picture too. Poor sleep affects coordination and reaction time at any age, and in older adults that can show up as a wobblier walk or a fall that seems to come out of nowhere. If balance has changed recently, sleep is worth bringing up with your doctor along with everything else you're already tracking.

## What to Track Now, and What to Bring to Your Next Appointment

You don't need special equipment to start noticing patterns. For two weeks, jot down when you went to bed, how many times you woke up, whether you needed the bathroom, and how rested you felt on a simple one to five scale. Note headaches, energy crashes, or naps you didn't plan to take.

A spouse, adult child, or anyone who shares your home often notices things you can't, like pauses in breathing, gasping, or restlessness. If someone else is around at night, ask what they've noticed over the past week or two.

Bring whatever you gather to your appointment, along with your questions. Our guide to [understanding your sleep study results](/sleep-study-results-explained/) walks through what those numbers actually mean if testing is already part of your picture.

## When a Sleep Evaluation Is Worth It

A sleep evaluation is worth considering when daytime fatigue, snoring changes, morning headaches, or frequent nighttime waking are affecting how you feel or function, even if you've assumed it's just your age. You don't need every symptom on a list. One or two that keep showing up is enough reason to ask.

Dr. Culotta's airway exam looks at tongue mobility and posture, palate width, jaw position, tonsil size, and whether you tend to breathe through your nose or your mouth at night, alongside a detailed health history. Our in-house [3D CBCT airway imaging](/services/cbct-airway-screenings/), an awake, upright scan that takes about 45 seconds, lets her see airway shape and volume directly. CBCT is not a sleep study and cannot diagnose sleep apnea on its own, but it gives a clearer starting point before you and a sleep physician talk about testing.

## Never Too Late: CPAP, Oral Appliances, and Options After 65

Age alone does not rule out treatment. Research on CPAP use in older adults has found that with the right support, people can adjust about as well as younger patients do. If a mask, hose, or nighttime bathroom trips made past attempts frustrating, a different mask style or added humidification can help, and CPAP remains an effective, well established first line option for many people.

### Oral Appliance Therapy, Even With Dentures or Missing Teeth

For some adults, especially those who have trouble tolerating CPAP, custom [oral appliance therapy](/services/sleep-appliances/) is worth exploring. Missing teeth or dentures do not automatically rule someone out. Dr. Culotta evaluates the health and position of remaining teeth, gum support, and jaw joints during a consultation to see whether oral appliance therapy is a good fit and what adjustments might be needed.

### A Note on Medicare

Medicare may help cover a physician ordered sleep study and a custom oral appliance for diagnosed obstructive sleep apnea when specific criteria, such as sleep study results and a doctor's order, are met. Coverage details vary by plan, so it's worth confirming with your insurance and asking our office what documentation is typically needed. ## Frequently Asked Questions

### Is It Too Late to Get Diagnosed With Sleep Apnea in My 70s or 80s?

It is not too late to be evaluated for sleep apnea at any age, and many older adults find real relief once breathing and sleep issues are addressed. Diagnosis starts with a conversation with your doctor and, usually, a sleep study, not an age cutoff.

### Why Don't I Snore Like I Used to, but I'm Still So Tired?

Snoring often becomes quieter with age even as sleep apnea continues or worsens, so a change in snoring doesn't mean the underlying issue improved. Fatigue, morning headaches, and trouble concentrating can be more telling signs later in life than loud snoring.

### Could My Memory or Mood Changes Be Related to Sleep Apnea?

They could be part of the picture, though memory and mood changes have many possible causes and deserve a full evaluation rather than a guess. Mentioning your sleep alongside your other symptoms gives your doctor a fuller picture to work from.

### Is CPAP Still a Good Option for Older Adults?

Yes, CPAP remains an effective, well established treatment for sleep apnea at any age, and many older adults use it successfully with the right adjustments. If a mask or hose has been uncomfortable in the past, different equipment or a fitting adjustment can often help.

### Can I Use an Oral Appliance If I Have Dentures or Missing Teeth?

Missing teeth or dentures do not automatically rule out oral appliance therapy, though the health and position of your remaining teeth and gums matter. Dr. Culotta evaluates your specific dental situation during a consultation to see what options fit.

### Does Medicare Cover Sleep Testing or Oral Appliance Therapy?

Medicare may help cover a physician ordered sleep study and a custom oral appliance for diagnosed obstructive sleep apnea when specific criteria are met. Coverage varies by plan, so it's worth confirming with your insurance and asking our office what documentation is typically needed.
